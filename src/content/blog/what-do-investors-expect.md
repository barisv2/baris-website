"So, how is your ARR?"

"Around 800k. We were at 500k last December."

"Have you seen Lovable? Zero to $2M in two months."

"Yeah. Good for them."

That was one of roughly 70 VC conversations we had over three months. Some went better. Some went worse. We closed $1.5M. Here's what I actually learned.

---

### Trajectory is the whole argument

VCs are pattern matchers. They're looking for one thing above everything else: Is this growing?

If the last few months point up, you're in the conversation. If they don't, no amount of polish gets you there. Or, of course, you can have a really successful exit and raise for another one without any trajectory, then, but you wouldn't be reading this, I guess.

---

### You only need one

The first term sheet is the hardest to get. Once one investor commits, the dynamic shifts completely. People who were "still evaluating" find clarity. Some who passed start asking for another call.

It's basically FOMO.

Don't try to close everyone at once. Find the one closest to yes, put your energy there, and get that first one. The rest tends to follow. But again, don't put all your eggs in the same basket

---

### Most VCs are followers

There are a handful of investors in any sector who others actually watch. When they move, it means something. Everyone else is mostly responding to the signal.

There are so many VCs who play safe. They rarely lead; you see them investing in startups with the same bunch of other VCs.

You don't need to be in every inbox. You need to be in the right ones. Figure out who leads in your space and start there. One yes from the right firm will bring 3-4 yeses from people waiting to see what happens.

---

### They all have filters. Learn them before you walk in.

Every investor has a mental checklist. Stage. Check size. Geography. Sector. Some won't touch anything below a certain ARR. Some don't do infrastructure. Some don't invest outside their home market.

Most of this is public. Their portfolio, their fund size, their thesis. Read it. If you clearly don't fit, don't send the email. If you do fit, lead with how.

We wasted a few weeks in conversations that were never going to close. Not because the company wasn't good enough. Because we didn't fit the filter.

---

### It's a sales process. But not only that.

Track your pipeline. Know where every conversation stands. Follow up consistently. This part is sales.

But the investors you take money from will be on your cap table for years. Their name is attached to yours. You're going to be sending them updates when things are bad. Some of them will be in the room when you're making big decisions.

We said no to some offers. The terms weren't terrible. The fit wasn't right. That's a valid reason.

---

### Don't stay in the gray area

This was our biggest mistake. I'm still a bit sad (sometimes) about it.

With a few VCs, we knew that there was a chance that we weren't going to move forward. We didn't say it. We kept it warm as a backup plan.

Say it early. Draw the lines and be strict about it. Know what you want. It's better than two more months of slow ghosting someone who is going to mention your name in a room you're not in.

---

### They talk to each other. Constantly.

Investors compare notes. They mention names. They talk to each other. All the time.

How you handled the conversation that didn't close matters almost as much as the one that did. Be consistent, be transparent, don't stretch your numbers. The ones you think are a no are still forming an impression.

It's harder to make them bid against them than you think. Or at least that's what I thought.

---

### You can push. Under two conditions

You can be pushy or be strict with your expectations and not negotiate. But not always. There are some scenarios where you can take things slow:

\#1 You can survive without the money. Default alive means you're negotiating from strength. The capital is wanted, not needed. That changes everything.

\#2 You have another offer on the table. Two investors wanting in is real leverage. Not fake urgency. Actual competing interest.

If neither is true, stay humble. Pushing hard from a weak position usually ends the same way.

---

### A few things that change the number

Not everyone raises at the same multiple. We could've raised with a higher valuation if: (also not 100% sure if this is a good thing)

\#1 If your core product is AI-native, valuations are running 3–5x higher right now. Its trendy.

\#2 If you have multinational revenue, add another 2–4x. It tells investors the product works outside one market. It reduces the biggest unknown in early-stage bets. Our revenue was mostly in Turkey, it didn't help.

---

### On timing

Our process took about three months. I've heard of companies closing in six weeks. I've heard of others going 16 months.

Don't use time as a KPI. The goal is to close the right round at the right terms. That happens when it happens. Focus on the quality of each conversation, the consistency of your updates, and keeping the trajectory pointing up.

I know it's draining, but try to enjoy it if you can. That's what we did.

---

### Newsletter helped a lot

We started sending monthly updates to anyone interested in Hardal's growth. Nearly a year before we started raising.

The constant communication and updates really helped when we decided to raise. People already knew how we were going, so basically the leads were warm.

100% recommended.
